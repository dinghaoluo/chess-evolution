# chess and work

Chess has a fixed objective and a cheap engine benchmark; a consultant's recommendation or a radiologist's diagnosis often needs evidence that is costly to obtain. The comparison therefore concerns particular decisions: whether the machine is reliably better at the task, and whether someone can check its output cheaply enough to correct it.

## workplace results

Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond's November 2024 v2 follows 5,172 customer-support agents through a staggered rollout: issues resolved per hour rise by about 15%, with speed and quality gains concentrated among less skilled workers (pp. 16–20). Top performers show small quality declines; outages supply evidence consistent with learning, although only handling time is measured at that frequency (pp. 25–26). Written English becomes more comprehensible and more native-like under assistance, measured by language-model ratings with human checks; retained unaided language proficiency is not tested (pp. 29–30, 61–63). Time savings are larger for topics rare to the agent, conditional on firm-wide frequency; globally, the largest gain occurs among moderately rare topics (pp. 27–28, Table A.9, p. 99).

Dell'Acqua et al.'s 2023 experiment with BCG consultants finds 12.2% more tasks completed and over 40% higher human-rated quality inside GPT-4's tested frontier (pp. 9–11, Tables 1–2, pp. 34–35). The 43% versus 17% comparison is improvement from each treated group's previous assessment, below versus above its average; it is a different contrast from the randomised treatment effect (p. 11). Outside the frontier, assisted consultants are nineteen percentage points less likely to give the correct answer (pp. 13–14, Table 4, p. 37). 'Centaurs' alternate human and AI work; 'cyborgs' integrate them within tasks, an observed typology whose relative effectiveness was not randomly assigned (p. 16, Appendix E).

Agarwal, Moehring, Rajpurkar, and Salz's original July 2023 paper gives AI predictions to radiologists reading chest X-rays: average diagnostic quality does not improve, despite useful machine information (pp. 23–27). Their behavioural model estimates underweighting of AI and neglect of dependence between signals; its preferred allocation usually delegates to the human or AI separately, conditional on specified error and time costs (pp. 36–44). The catalogue retains the July 2023 original and August 2026 revision; these page references belong to the original.

Budzyń et al. compare 795 non-AI colonoscopies before exposure with 648 afterwards, across nineteen experienced endoscopists: adenoma detection falls from 28.4% to 22.4%, a difference of −6.0 percentage points (95% CI −10.5 to −1.6; WRAP Table 2, p. 27). Exposure was not randomly assigned for this comparison; a screening-programme suspension and altered diagnostic workload coincide with the change (pp. 18–19). The study establishes an association with poorer unassisted detection.

Vaccaro, Almaatouq, and Malone combine 106 experiments from 74 papers: human–AI performance exceeds humans alone, Hedges' g = 0.64 (95% CI 0.53–0.74), yet falls below the better partner, g = −0.23 (−0.39 to −0.07; pp. 2294–2295). The latter difference is positive when humans alone are stronger, g = 0.46, and negative when AI alone is stronger, g = −0.54 (p. 2296); this comparison across experiments cannot date the closure of an occupation's collaboration window.

## limits of the transfer

Chess participation and short-run task performance do not measure employment effects or social welfare; neither outcome establishes how gains are distributed or how demand responds. Historical chess curves also leave individual exposure unobserved: randomised workplace contrasts support causal claims about their assigned interventions, without identifying engines as the cause of chess's forty-year trajectory.
